# Who we help, what they fear, and what actually works

Research done 2026-10-27 to steer what Fall 0 shows and advises. The advice the cards give lives in
`src/fall_zero/skill/catalog/fixes.toml`; this file says why.

## Who gets hurt, and how

- About **17,800 emergency-room injuries a year** in the US from TVs, furniture and appliances tipping over
  (2020–2022). There were 217 deaths reported from 2013 to July 2023. [CPSC 2023 report]
- **Children: 71% of deaths.** 55% of all deaths are children aged 1–3. Where the scenario is known, most
  children who died were **climbing** the piece; the rest were pulling on it. [CPSC]
- **Older adults (60+): 26% of deaths, 39% of the deaths where only furniture fell, and 24% of injuries.**
  21% of injured seniors are hospitalized, against 3% of children. They pull furniture over when they
  **grab it or lean on an open drawer for balance**. [CPSC; CPSC Anchor It tip card for older adults; NCOA]
- **TVs are in 47% of deaths.** A TV on a dresser is the classic case: the child climbs the drawers to
  reach it. Chests, bureaus and dressers are in 36% of deaths. [CPSC]
- **44% of deaths happen in bedrooms.** Half the victims were crushed and stayed under the piece, and two
  thirds of the fatal injuries were to the head. [CPSC]
- **Earthquakes:** falling and moving furniture caused **30–50% of injuries** in recent Japanese
  earthquakes, and upper floors move furniture more. [Tokyo Fire Department]

## What people believe, and fear

- Two thirds of Americans don't anchor their furniture. Their reasons: **"it seems stable enough"**
  (41–45%), **no holes in the wall** (25–29%, a renter's worry), no hardware came with it, and not knowing
  what to buy. [Consumer Reports surveys 2018, 2021]
- Many parents think **watching is enough**. CPSC's "Even when you're watching" campaign answers that a
  tip-over takes seconds. [CPSC]
- What they fear most is a toddler crushed or hit on the head, the TV coming down, and it happening at
  night in the child's room.

## What they want to know

1. Is *my* piece actually dangerous, or is it heavy enough?
2. Which one do I fix first?
3. What exactly do I buy, what does it cost, how long does it take, and how do I do it?
4. I rent: can I do it without drilling, and does that work?
5. Does the kit that came with it work?
6. How far from the crib, bed or play mat?
7. Is my dresser recalled?

## What actually works

- **Metal or nylon straps screwed into a stud, two per piece.** In Consumer Reports' tests these held
  230–350 lb per anchor. A plastic strap pulled out at 23 lb, and peel-and-stick kits did worst. A screw
  in bare drywall pulls out. [Consumer Reports anchor-kit tests 2024]
- **TVs:** strap the TV to the wall or to an anchored stand, or mount it. Use a low, sturdy stand with the
  TV pushed back. [CPSC Anchor It]
- **Habits:**
  - Keep nothing tempting (toys, the remote, snacks) on top.
  - Put the heaviest things low.
  - Fit drawer latches.
  - Keep cribs, beds and play areas out of where it would land.
  - Check for recalls.
  - [CPSC Anchor It]
- **Older adults:** keep everyday things within reach, never on top of furniture or the TV, and replace
  anchoring devices as needed. [CPSC tip card for older adults]
- **Earthquakes:**
  - L-brackets into the wall.
  - Where you can't drill, tension poles to the ceiling with stopper mats under the front.
  - Latches on cabinet doors.
  - Tall furniture away from beds, doorways and hallways.
  - [Tokyo Fire Department; Shinjuku City]

## What this means for the product

- **Show the belief failing.** The simulation tips the "stable enough" piece. It shows how quickly it
  falls (answering "I'm watching") and where it lands: the crib, the play mat.
- **Show people, not test weights:** a child climbing the drawers, a grandparent grabbing the top.
- **Every fix is practical:**
  - what to buy, the cost, the time and the tools;
  - three steps;
  - what to avoid (plastic straps, peel-and-stick, bare drywall);
  - an honest renter option.
- **Rank by who's at risk:** bedrooms, TVs, dressers, climbing. Ask only the question that would change
  the answer, such as whether it's strapped, which floor, or what's in the drawers.
